Allergies Explained: Causes, Symptoms, Diagnosis and Prevention

What happens during an allergy

An allergy begins when the immune system treats a normally harmless substance as a threat. Pollen, dust mites, animal proteins, foods and medications are familiar triggers. The body may react through different immune pathways. Many immediate allergies involve IgE antibodies and the release of histamine, which can produce itching, swelling and other symptoms.

Why symptoms differ

Allergies do not look the same in everyone. Hay fever often brings sneezing, congestion and itchy eyes. Contact allergies can cause a delayed rash. Food allergies may involve hives, vomiting or breathing problems. Similar symptoms can arise from non-allergic conditions, so a useful diagnosis requires more than spotting one symptom.

Who is more likely to develop allergies

Family history, eczema and factors affecting immune development can contribute to risk. The environment may also play a part, although no single exposure explains all allergies. A parent with hay fever does not guarantee their child will develop the same condition. Allergies may first appear in childhood or adulthood.

How clinicians investigate

A clinician starts by asking when symptoms started and what happened just beforehand. Selected skin-prick and blood tests may help investigate immediate allergies. Patch tests are used for certain delayed contact reactions. A positive result alone does not prove clinical allergy; it has to fit the history.

Treatment depends on the trigger

Limiting confirmed exposures may help reduce symptoms. Antihistamines, nasal steroid sprays and eye drops can improve particular conditions. Specialist-directed immunotherapy is an option for some diagnosed allergies. Serious reactions involving breathing difficulty or collapse require emergency care, and prescribed epinephrine should be used according to an individual action plan.

What prevention really means

There is no guaranteed way to prevent every allergy. Routine avoidance of major foods during infancy is not generally recommended once a baby is ready for solids, although high-risk infants may need tailored advice. Good skin care and sensible environmental measures can support comfort. Explore why allergies happen and how testing works.

A note about individual differences

The most useful plan depends on your symptoms, other health conditions and personal circumstances. Keeping a brief record of when a problem starts and what makes it better or worse can help you and your clinician decide what to investigate next. Avoid assuming that the experience of another person will be identical to yours.
